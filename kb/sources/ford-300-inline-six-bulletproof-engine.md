## Summary

An overview of the Ford 300 (4.9L) inline-six — the engine in this truck — covering its
history, design, and why it earned a "bulletproof" reputation. The 300 was part of Ford's
straight-six family produced **1965–1996**, with over 4 million built, primarily at the
Cleveland engine plant. "300" is the displacement in cubic inches (4.9 L metric). It's a
simple **overhead-valve (OHV)** cast-iron design prized for low-end torque, durability, and
ease of maintenance, used across F-series trucks, Broncos, E-series vans, and industrial and
agricultural equipment.

Key design traits: a long stroke and relatively low compression (≈8:1–9:1) that produce
**peak torque down low, around 1,600–2,200 RPM**, ideal for towing and hauling. Firing order
is **1-5-3-6-2-4**. The cast-iron block makes it heavy (~500–550 lb). Over its run it gained
hardened valve seats (late 1970s, for unleaded fuel), **EFI in the 1980s** (the "EFI 300"
that powers 1980s–90s trucks like this 1994), and a move from points to electronic ignition.
It is a workhorse, not a high-performance engine — the narrator is even swapping his for a V8,
acknowledging the 300's torque-and-reliability character doesn't chase horsepower.

## Key Points

- Produced **1965–1996**; >4 million built; successor to the 262 cu in six.
- **300 cu in = 4.9 L**, OHV inline-six, cast-iron block and head, ~500–550 lb.
- Compression ≈ 8:1–9:1; **peak torque ≈ 1,600–2,200 RPM**; firing order **1-5-3-6-2-4**.
- Late-1970s: hardened valve seats for unleaded fuel.
- **1980s: EFI** ("EFI 300") + electronic ignition — the configuration in a 1994 F-150.
- Reputation is built on **low-end torque + durability + simplicity**, not peak power.
