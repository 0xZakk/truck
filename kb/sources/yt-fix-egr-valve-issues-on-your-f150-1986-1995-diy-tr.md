## Summary

A DIY diagnosis of a check-engine light on a 1994 F-150 that turned out to be a vacuum-leak
problem rather than a failed EGR valve. The presenter notes that the auto-parts store scanner
returned "no codes" on this older EEC-IV truck, so he pulled the codes through the
on-board flashing-code method (the check-engine lamp blinks the two-digit code) and got a
**code 332 — an EGR problem**. He explains that the position sensor on top of the EGR valve
throws 332 when it detects no movement of the valve, and that valve movement is driven by
vacuum delivered through a control solenoid.

The troubleshooting is a methodical vacuum trace. He puts a vacuum gauge on the line at the
EGR valve and sees no movement, then works back to the EGR vacuum control solenoid and finds
the manifold-vacuum supply (the white line) is also dead, confirming a leak somewhere upstream
rather than a bad valve or solenoid. Following the lines back toward the EVAP canister, he
finds a cracked vacuum hose hidden under a heat shield. He repairs it with a cheap plastic
splice/union from the parts store.

After the splice he re-verifies the fix: manifold vacuum now reads strong at the solenoid's
white line, and a slight-but-real needle deflection appears at the EGR valve, confirming the
solenoid is passing vacuum through to the valve. A road test leaves the check-engine light off,
so the 332 is resolved without replacing any parts. The takeaway is to chase vacuum leaks
before condemning the EGR valve or solenoid.

## Key Points

- The complaint was a **code 332** (EGR-related) on a 1994 F-150 4.9L EEC-IV truck.
- Generic parts-store scanners may report **"no codes"** on these older trucks; read codes via
  the on-board flashing-lamp self-test instead.
- 332 is thrown by the **EGR position sensor** when it detects no valve movement; movement
  comes from vacuum routed through the EGR control solenoid.
- Diagnose with a **vacuum gauge**: the working signal at the EGR valve is very slight, so watch
  the needle closely; warm the engine and blip the throttle to pull good manifold vacuum.
- Trace vacuum back through the **control solenoid** (white line = manifold supply, green line =
  to the valve) and on toward the **EVAP canister**.
- Root cause here was a **cracked vacuum hose hidden under a heat shield**, fixed with a cheap
  plastic splice — no EGR valve or solenoid replacement needed.
