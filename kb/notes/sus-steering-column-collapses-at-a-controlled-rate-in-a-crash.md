The F-150 steering column is an energy-absorbing assembly engineered to crush progressively in
a collision instead of spearing the wheel rearward at the driver. Several jacket constructions
do this: a slip-tube held by plastic inserts or rivets that shear, or a slotted/corrugated
mesh or bellows section that shortens under load. The two-piece shift tube is bonded with
plastic shear pins that are pared away by a knife edge in the adjoining tube as the column
compresses.

The two-piece steering shaft telescopes against the resistance of plastic injections; crucially
its solid upper section has a double-flatted lower end that mates into the hollow lower piece,
so steering still works even after the shaft has fully collapsed. The mounting bracket adds a
second stage: two breakaway capsules let the bracket slip off its attaching points so the whole
column can yield forward if the driver is thrown into it. All of this is why column parts must
be handled and reassembled carefully — bent or substituted parts defeat the crash function of
the `steering` system.
